Craggenrock postmortem

“I wish I knew back then the things that I know now.” This has been the main thought in my head for months after Craggenrock was released. “I have a lot of things I’ve learned recently. If only the former me had known them, I wouldn’t have failed.”

If only things were that convenient… 

  • Game Title: Craggenrock
  • Genre: Simulation
  • Release Platform/s: PC (Steam)
  • Release Date: 21 July 2025
  • Developer: Ardeimon

Craggenrock’s release, to put it mildly, has been… less than stellar. Things certainly could have gone a whole lot better, but they didn’t. There were a lot of things I could have done, I admit. Now that it’s been a little over a year since its release, I’ve had plenty of time to recover, reflect on things, and analyze what I could improve in the future.

Developing the game

How it started

Craggenrock started as a project that stemmed from my love of discovering crafting recipes from Divinity: Original Sin way back in 2017. It had been a year since I graduated from college, and I was still trying to discover what I wanted to do with my life. One quiet night shortly after playing D:OS, I started compiling ideas for a crafting game.

Back then, I still hadn't dabbled in game development, but I knew I had to at least try documenting what I wanted in a game that involved crafting.

Some pages of Craggenrock's earliest GDD dating back to Oct. 2017 

I was a huge RPG fan, so the first few bits I wrote were focused more on the main character and the story of the game I wanted. As days went by, I compiled more stuff. I did my research on crafting games and, while there were some, none of them really felt like they had crafting at the forefront. They just didn’t do it for me. I thought I could probably take a shot at it, but without any skills or experience, I knew it was just a pipe dream; a game of that magnitude was too much. So I shelved it.

One of the earliest builds dating back to 2018. Just walking. No mechanics yet.

Years passed, and it wasn’t until 2021 that I finally un-shelved the then-crafting RPG idea I had. At that point, I already had some game dev experience from game jams. I realized my idea was way too big, so I stripped it down to feature just the crafting. I took ideas from my old stories in my teenage years to form the setting and premise. It took me months, but I slowly began developing it bit by bit as I also learned the ropes of game development past game jams. I had to learn pixel art, game design, extensive programming, sound design, etc. All on my own. The thought of looking for teammates crossed my mind only briefly ‘cause, frankly, I had no money. Who would wanna work for free for something they weren’t sure was gonna sell?

Old logo and names of Craggenrock. More about them in the tidbits post.

Streak of good luck...?

2022 came and I happened upon the most important opportunity of my life at that point: getting included in the Philippine Indie Games Initiative (Project PIGI). It was an initiative to mentor local indie devs. I wasn’t sure I’d get in, but I took my shot thinking I wouldn’t lose anything anyway if I didn't. But then I got it. During its run, we got to learn how to make a pitch deck and practice pitching to publishers. We got into talks and even got the opportunity to showcase our games at a gaming convention. A game that I was making managed to get that far. In a way, my idea was validated. It made me feel like the game was worth pursuing.

Then came 2023. During the Electronic Sports and Gaming Summit (ESGS) that year, I landed an award. Things felt like they were going great. When 2024 arrived, I also joined the first season of the Young Creatives Challenge (YC2), a nationwide contest for creatives and their works. I didn't really expect things to go as well as they did, but I went as far as landing 1st runner-up.

Defense day for YC2. Felt like a more intense thesis defense.

I couldn't understand how I managed to get that far up the rankings. When the 2nd runner-up was announced, leaving 2 more spots, my heart rate shot up like crazy. In my head, I was afraid I actually had a chance of getting first place. It felt so wrong. There were plenty of what I thought were more polished games compared to mine, and yet there I was, getting that far. I didn't want the attention, the pressure, that feeling that I needed to prove that my game was deserving of its rank. When my studio and game finally got announced as the 1st runner-up, I was all sorts of emotions inside. Ultimately, I felt relieved I didn't place too high. (My family mentioned my face looked really weird when I went up on stage. This is why.)

An early interview during PGDX 2024.
Credits and many thanks to Pinoygamer for the opportunity.
Rocky road all over

Months went by, and I had to, or so I felt, "live up" to the expectations from that series of good performances. I thought I just had to. Somehow, with a barebones team of 1, I had to triple the effort and make the game worth a couple of awards.

An old(?) version of Craggenrock's pitch deck. Could swear I got it down to just 10 or 12 slides. 

Over the course of its development, I had some opportunities to pitch Craggenrock to some publishers. A few took months of back and forth, but to no avail. The rejections and ghosting, extended amounts of time preparing while working on the game, combined with a messed-up sleeping schedule, diet, extended social isolation, and poor overall mental health. All of it took a heavy toll on me.

There have been multiple times when I've burnt myself out working on the game. That or my depressive episodes where I just felt like everything was all for nothing. In those moments when I wasn't at my peak, I still felt that I needed to keep on moving. There was guilt about not being productive and not doing enough. That feeling never really went away; it wanes, but it's always there.

In the months leading up to the game's release, I still hadn't reached a state comfortable enough to work. However, instead of resting, I used that guilt as fuel for a last push. In my mind, I just needed to release the game as soon as I possibly could. Release first, rest (and deal with consequences) later. Bahala na.

The release and aftermath

The only photo of Craggenrock's release moment. Just enough for a story, I guess. 🤷‍♂️

Truth be told, I had set the release date of my game in Steam to be much earlier than it ended up being. I missed that date by several months. (I can't remember what it was, unfortunately.) By the time I pressed the Release button on Steam, I was already way off my target.

I had delayed releasing the game multiple times, thinking I could "do more, fix more until it's ready." I didn't know that feeling never goes away, like ever. I think what finally made me get over that feeling was fatigue; I was just too tired of everything. I just wanted it to be over.

I've seen some studios do their game's releases with parties together with their team or loved ones. I did mine at 1 AM in my room alone with my dogs and a cup of coffee, maybe. Such an important event, and yet I don't remember it to be as momentous as I would have wanted.

The first 2 weeks of the game's release were hectic. People were reporting issues left and right while, at the time, I also had to prepare for the Philippine Game Dev Expo (PGDX). Things got a whole lot more overwhelming when my family experienced a loss. Still sleepless from crunching, I didn't know what to prioritize.

What they say about a game being at its peak during the first week and/or month of its release was all so true. By the time I was finally able to work things out, the craze was over. That was it essentially for Craggenrock.

It's been more than a year since its release. I'm afraid not much else changed. It's safe to say it's done for.

Internalizing things

...Unfortunately, I'm not willing to share the other juicy numbers. I still find it difficult and embarrassing how massive this... eff-up turned out to be. But, to give you a rough idea of how much I've earned from it, it's barely enough to reach a livable wage to get you through 3 months here in the Philippines, at least.

Where it went wrong

Where did it not go wrong, amirite?

Poor marketing and management

To no one's surprise.

I admit this is probably my biggest fault. I did not share word about the game enough. I think it's a combination of imposter syndrome and poor overall management. Considering the state I was in, it's not too difficult to see how I was probably overwhelmed with the workload, leading to poor decisions and mismanaged time and effort.

I always found it difficult coming up with things to post and share despite having, what I see now as, a lot of stuff I could tell others about. I always resorted to thinking "This isn't good enough to post," which ended up with me sharing barely anything. The number of things I've posted is laughably countable.

To add to this, I also didn't know about marketing beats and how milestones can play into those. I was late to realize why others do multiple trailers and how details/info are breadcrumbed. I also failed to factor in the amount of time and effort needed to prepare before releasing in general. (The tediousness of building a creator spreadsheet, don't get me started.) I did absolutely nothing when I entered Craggenrock on the Steam Next Fest, for example. (I'll probably make a post about this in the future.)

For you: 
- For the love of everything, LET THE WORLD KNOW YOUR GAME EXISTS.
- Posts don't always need to be pretty. They can be funny, relatable, informational, etc. Anything is WAY better than absolutely nothing. Even if no one reacts, it's still proof that the game is alive.
- There's always someone who's working on something better than you. That's a given. Instead of focusing your energy on something that is unlikely to change, work on something you CAN change. You will never beat those "better" works of theirs, but you can always beat your game's worst record if you work on it now. (e.g. which is better: 42 errors left or 38 errors left?)
- Study what other devs are doing with their marketing. Observe patterns in what they're doing. Absorb everything, not just the viral/trending parts. They wouldn't go viral if they didn't do some groundwork. That groundwork is what you need, most likely.
- In your spare time, start finding content creators probably worth your game's time. Ignore subscriber count and views (for the newbie devs).
Gigantic scope vs. party of 1

There are some great people who can accomplish great things on their own. I don't think I am one of those people... yet(?).

I might have tried my best to scope everything down to the core of what I wanted (just the crafting), but I still completely overestimated what is a reliably "good" scope for one person. Someone who practically had to learn everything from scratch, at that. Wearing the hats of multiple roles at once, aiming to squeeze in as much work in a 24-hour timeframe while taking into account personal needs, with the body and mind of just one person is such a huuuuuge undertaking. The life of a solo indie dev being hard is one big understatement, to say the least. Looking back, it's quite difficult to imagine how my old self somehow managed to get through everything that had happened. (I still came out pretty beat up, but well...)

There was just too much to go through. In hindsight, shortcomings were bound to happen. It could have gone better, yes, but also possibly a lot worse. This was a fair, though deeply unfortunate, compromise.

Too much, too soon

If we really think about it, the game's unfortunate turnout is a result of me biting off more than I could chew. This is more than me being solo, being a newbie, overwhelmed, etc. This is everything combined.

I simply went in big, without enough knowledge and the experience needed to handle things reasonably well. I might have tried my best learning from other devs, reading up on all sorts of articles and attending workshops about game development, but even then, nothing beats just plain old experiencing it firsthand.

I could have learned everything that I know now, trickled to me, through smaller projects finished in probably much shorter, more manageable timeframes over the course of all those years. I could have done a much better job pitching to publishers if I had experience reaching out to them and knowing how it goes. I could have planned releasing a demo for Next Fest with everything that I needed if only I knew how it is supposed to be. I could have taken more rests if only I knew I was pushing myself beyond what was humanly reasonable.

I really went all in as a newbie with Craggenrock. That much I know now. All of this knowledge and "Now I know"s came crushing down on me like a waterfall instead of light jabs. I've come to realize all this in talks with my therapist a few months back. I really can't fault my old self for his decisions and actions back then 'cause he was working with nothing, from nothing. Now that I'm much more equipped and battle-hardened than my former self, I've got a whole lot more stuff to prepare me in the future.

I will still fail, let's be honest. But now, it won't go as hard as I did here. (Hopefully...)

For you:
There really is a lot I can say about this, but to avoid going all too personal and mushy: "To err is human; to forgive, divine". Remember the human in you.
What went right

There's still a silver lining behind all of this, thankfully. I might have taken a long time to realize them, but here they are.

Strong (or decent) visuals

The game does take some inspiration from Moonlighter, among multiple references.

The most common compliment Craggenrock receives is its great art style. Plenty have noted its "cute" and "colorful" visuals, and comparisons to Moonlighter are through the roof. As a fan of the OG Moonlighter and other games with beautiful pixel art, I'm glad I did great here. Years of learning how to do art feel like it actually paid off.

I am thankful that research and experimentation got Craggenrock at least this much. I can't help but wonder how I'll fare venturing into other styles possibly in the future.

For you: Do your research.
Landing on an art style is a balance of what fits your game and what you can achieve as the developer. I think it should be at the center.

For those who are still starting out, especially those who do not have the art skills (or resources) yet, the "what you can achieve" part is incredibly important. It factors in how much/long you are willing to learn and/or spend. Since you're new, you may not have enough for experimentation; efficiency is the key. It's easy to think "I want X art style for my game," but you also need to think "what happens if it doesn't work out?" You'll be scrapping art, money, time, and effort. I think it would help a lot to base your decisions for art style on your ability (or financial capability) as a beginner, even more so during the early stages of your game's development where a lot of things can change.

For the more capable devs, you might already have years of experience (or plenty of resources) to figure out the part you can do. Competitor research, just keep it in mind.
As cozy as it can be

What little boost it had was thanks to the cozy trend, though it was somehow coincidental. Even before the likes of cozy farming games and cleaning simulators became popular, I'd already been a fan of slice-of-life anime, which had a very laidback pacing. I was already beginning to rescope the game, taming its planned serious tone too, when the cozy genre was picking up pace. I decided to lean into the cozy vibe and it worked to some extent.

The game may not have pulled enough given its marketing, but the ones that discovered and played it did so due to having that "cozy" tag. During conventions, the game regularly got comments about it being laidback, sometimes a bit too much.

Foot (and leg) in the door

Things may not have turned out too well, but I did end up getting some tools under my belt. For starters, I've built up a lot of experience when it comes to releasing the game, everything from how to more efficiently start to more effectively handling things behind what players see. That much is irreplaceable.

Years of joining conventions, getting my game out there and participating in other events also led me to meet some amazing people. I went from a nobody to someone at least a few people could recognize. There was even a time where I got a chance to be in a masterclass with previous mentors, a scenario I never thought was possible.

Lastly, I'm not really one to toot my own horn, but I did bag some awards. If anything, they're proof that I can accomplish something worth recognizing. Enough said...?

For you:
Don't be afraid to get yourselves out there. I know some people (like me) are a bit more introverted, but you'd be surprised at how easy it is to talk to other people who really love making and playing games. It genuinely feels like meeting fellow children in a playground. Once you meet someone who plays the same games as you, you're automatically friends. Fun!
What now?

Honestly, I don't know. This is currently where I'm at, though I think I'm on the cusp of breaking through. I can feel it.

I'm torn between releasing one or two more updates to Craggenrock as a sort of sendoff and maybe moving on to something else. There's a sentimental part of me that thinks there's still something I can do about the game, that it still hasn't reached its full potential, but we all know that feeling may never fully go away. The other logical part of me thinks that spending more time and effort on the game is worthless, seeing there's no more financial gain or interest coming from it. It's a constant tug-of-war. I don't know when it'll end.

I guess we'll all see. 😮‍💨

 

To greener pastures and beyond

If I’m being completely honest, I do not like the feeling of being another cautionary tale out of many. However, I’m sharing this hoping that it could at least give something to those who are planning to go into game dev the way I did. My tale is a good example showing that if things can go wrong, they will. It’d be best to take precautions and think of things as extensively as you can.

Of course, if things go differently for you, then congratulations, genuinely. 

To the future me who’d be unlikely to read this again, if you are, I hope things have turned out for the better. While Craggenrock might have fallen short of our expectations, it is still our darling worth loving. 

To the readers, I hope you are well. Please take care of yourselves, and the best of luck to you.

This is not a goodbye. We will see each other again soon, probably on Bluesky, Instagram, or wherever. ✌️
